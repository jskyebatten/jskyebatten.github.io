About Me

When I walked into my first university CS course, I was expecting the collaborative classroom that had made me fall in love with computing in high school: peer-led, hands-on, brainstorming with my fellow peers. Instead I encountered quiet classrooms echoing with the booming voices of professors, mired in an unspoken culture of competition. I looked around and counted four other women. I almost switched to nursing.

I didn't leave, but I never stopped thinking about the students who did.

I ultimately stayed in CS because I stumbled across a community of researchers who were asking the same questions I couldn't stop asking (shoutout to the LEARN-CS Lab + Dr. Ross!). That community gave me a sense of belonging, and it showed me that changing the culture of computing education wasn't something I could do alone. It would take a community of researchers working together. Here I am almost 10 years later, still studying the dynamic nature of computing education. And boy is it ever-changing.

My Work

One question has driven all of my work:

How do we improve computing higher education so that it works for everyone?

I study the forces, visible and invisible, that push minoritized students out of computing. The policies disguised as rigor. The implicit messages embedded in how professors teach. The classroom cultures that signal who belongs and who doesn't. I'm interested in the stuff that gets overlooked as just structural features of higher education, because those are often the features doing the most damage.

Answering this question has meant working across multiple scales. At the classroom level, I've conducted ethnographic research inside introductory programming courses, examining how implicit messages about ability shape female student engagement. At the institutional level, I've studied how transfer students navigate the transition into computing degree programs and what makes that pathway work or fall apart. And at the national level, I now work with longitudinal enrollment data from 150+ computing departments, tracking how the field is shifting in real time.

Moving between these scales, from how a single professor talks about programming concepts, to how a department structures its degree programs, to how enrollment patterns are changing nationally, is what I think makes my work distinctive. These levels aren't separate problems. What happens inside a classroom is shaped by departmental policy, which is shaped by field-wide trends.

Right now, computing education is in the middle of a significant shift. Enrollment in computing undergraduate and graduate degree programs are declining. Departments across the country are creating new AI and data science programs in response, but it's still unclear whether these changes actually serve students or simply follow market pressure. I think the researchers who understand the human side of computing education, how students experience these environments, how identity and belonging are shaped by them, are exactly who needs to be at the table as institutions figure out what comes next.
